01 — How it works

Light in. Numbers out.

A barcode is just a pattern of dark and light. Everything else is measurement. Here is the whole chain, in five steps — no physics background required.

reflected light absorbed light measured brightness
1

Light shines

An LED in the micro:bit’s 5 × 5 matrix is briefly reversed and used as a photodiode. It emits a tiny amount of light and measures what comes back.

2

White reflects

White spaces in the barcode reflect most of the light back towards the sensor. The reading goes up.

3

Black absorbs

Black bars absorb most of the light instead of reflecting it. Very little comes back, so the reading drops.

4

Sensor measures

As the sensor moves across the code, the matrix reports a brightness value between 0 and 255 for every position.

5

Signal becomes data

The changing brightness is turned into a binary pattern. Measuring how wide each bar and space is gives us the 13 digits.

05 — Signal processing

From a wobbly line to thirteen digits

The sensor has no idea what a barcode is. It only reports brightness. Everything below happens in software — and every step is visible.

1

Thresholding

An Otsu threshold splits the readings into “dark” and “light”. No fixed magic number — the threshold adapts to the actual lighting.

180 175 178 → 1 1 1
35 40 38 → 0 0 0
2

Noise filtering

A 3-sample median filter removes single-sample spikes caused by hand wobble and sensor noise.

[178, 240, 176] → [178, 176, 176]
3

Run-length encoding

Instead of storing every sample, we store how many samples each bar or space lasted. That length is what actually carries the data.

■4 □4 ■8 □4 ■12 □4 …
4

Bar & space widths

The full barcode is exactly 95 modules wide. Dividing the total width by 95 gives the width of one module, so each run becomes 1–4 modules.

total 380 px ÷ 95 = 4.0 px / module
5

EAN-13 decoding

Guards at 101 / 01010 / 101 anchor the code. Six left digits use L/G parity to encode the first digit; six right digits use R codes. A checksum confirms it.

101 | 6×7 | 01010 | 6×7 | 101

06 — The science

Three ideas make this work

Each of these is something you could explain on a napkin. Together they turn a stripe of ink into a number.

Light reflection

A white surface bounces most of the light back. A black surface absorbs it and warms up by a tiny, tiny amount instead. That difference in reflected light is the entire barcode — printed in ink, read as brightness.

Optical sensors

An LED can also absorb light. Photons knock electrons loose, a tiny current flows, and an analogue-to-digital converter turns that current into a number from 0 to 255. That number is all we ever get.

01100101
10011010
00110111
11001000

Binary code

Computers cannot store “quite bright”. They store 1s and 0s. Above the threshold we write 1, below it we write 0. The pattern of those bits is the barcode, and the widths of the runs of bits are the digits.

How the pieces fit together

The original build runs this pipeline in Python on a laptop. This website implements the identical pipeline in JavaScript so judges can try it straight from the browser.

Our innovation

Instead of buying a laser scanner, we use the LED matrix of a BBC micro:bit as a light sensor. The micro:bit measures brightness while it is moved across a printed barcode, and the resulting signal is decoded into a real EAN-13 number in software.
